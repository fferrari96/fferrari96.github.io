In brief

I develop and characterize thin-film processes — deposition, patterning, doping — and build the test setups that close the loop: vary the process, measure, relate the results statistically, set the next experiment. Seven years of cleanroom and lab work on semiconductor films and devices, and a PhD in Applied Physics from Groningen.

Along the way I showed that mixing time alone shifts the conductivity of n-doped films by orders of magnitude, developed a way to dope them without vacuum, and built an automated rig that measures four regions of a sample at once instead of one. Most recently, at AilumiQ in Taipei, I fabricated photodiode stacks for short-wave infrared detectors and built the first version of a high-speed optoelectronic test bench.

Why science

Growing up watching popular science channels, I discovered the joy of learning something unexpected and understanding something counterintuitive. Curiosity became my inner compass but, instead of pointing in one direction, it made everything interesting. That is why I chose to study Materials Science in Padova: being in the middle between chemistry, physics and even biology, it was for me the program that allowed me to keep exploring wide but also in depth.

Why research

After the bachelor I really wanted to see research up close, and maybe contribute to it. I ended up at KAUST, which felt like a playground for scientists: the facilities were brand new, and access to all kinds of equipment enabled a great deal of creative freedom. I joined the Organic Bioelectronics Lab, and that's where I first got familiar with thin-film deposition and lithography, and built my first automated setup.

Once that period ended, my itch to go into depth was stronger than ever, so I decided to do a PhD in Groningen, in a group led by a physicist, starting with how moving a glycol chain around a fullerene changes its properties.

Thin films and semiconductor devices are my core expertise: physical vapor deposition (thermal evaporation, sputtering, e-beam), spin coating, photolithography, dry etching and surface preparation, followed by structural and electrical characterization. I run process development as a closed loop: vary the process variables systematically, measure, relate the results statistically in Python, and set the next experiment. That is how we found that mixing time alone shifts conductivity by orders of magnitude, and how I developed a vacuum-free doping process.

Why engineering

During my PhD I was often limited by my experimental setups: I couldn't study what I felt was important in the right way. That's when I realized that to become the scientist I wanted to be, I first needed to become a good engineer.

Also, I just enjoy the process of translating an idea into something concrete, rather than just data. And each feature you decide to implement requires you to learn something new, so I got to learn about electronics, firmware, mechanics and a bit of fluidics too! The conductivity rig below came out of that.

Projects

Building the rig I wished I had

The rig started from a frustration with how we prepared and measured samples. Our standard device layout wasted a lot of expensive substrate: most of the materials I worked with were either costly or synthesized by organic chemists in batches under 100 mg, yet the layout gave us only two regions to measure conductivity and one for the Seebeck coefficient. On top of that, every device was measured one at a time on a probe station — repetitive and slow, despite the layout having stayed the same for years.

So I redesigned the shadow mask used to pattern the electrodes — trying waterjet cutting, CNC milling, and spark erosion along the way — and built a rig around it that would let me measure more of a sample, automatically.

The clearest case for it came from the vapor-doping paper: I wanted to see whether the distribution of dopant changed the kinetics of activation, which meant annealing a sample while recording its conductivity — pressing a button every two minutes for several hours, for one device per run. The new setup automates that measurement and reads four regions at once, so a run that used to return a single data point now returns four.

First-Author Paper · Advanced Electronic Materials 2026

Chemistry ahead, physics catching up

The premise of this study is what makes it interesting. Fullerene derivatives are an intriguing class of materials: their chemistry is vast — thousands of derivatives have been synthesized, and there is no shortage of reviews — but the solid-state physics of these materials is far less explored, leaving many gaps in the structure–property picture. In this work we probe one such gap: how simply changing where a glycol chain sits on the molecule affects a wide variety of material properties — from the dielectric constant to the mobility, the morphology and the doping efficiency.

This study was very fun, exactly for that reason: it was a playground to explore and learn, hands on, the different characterization methods. Perfect for a first PhD project, since it let me get familiar with all the tools available in the lab.

What I really liked was getting to fabricate unipolar Schottky diodes and metal–insulator–semiconductor devices to characterize the mobility and the charge-carrier concentration of these materials. The results were anything but boring: the as-cast mobilities of the three isomers spanned more than an order of magnitude; annealing made them all drop — more order is not always better for transport; and the dielectric constants ranged from about 4.5 to 8 depending on the isomer and the measurement frequency.

Another really exciting technique was performing GIWAXS on the thin films. The diffractograms of these materials are really beautiful — the one of the 2,5 isomer looks like the starry ceiling of the firmament that Giotto painted in the Scrovegni Chapel Giotto · Scrovegni Chapel, c. 1305 Gold stars on a lapis-blue vault, Padova. , in my hometown Padova. Beyond the aesthetics, this is a dataset I am quite happy about: it allowed me to perform indexing, which is not very common when working with polymer-based organic semiconductors, where diffraction patterns are usually too diffuse. Indexing told the packing stories: the 2,5 isomer grows a low-symmetry triclinic lattice, highly crystalline; the other two organize in tetragonal cells of alternating fullerene and glycol layers — the packing that in-plane transport likes best.

In the end, this structure–property mapping is what let us push the best isomer to a conductivity of 7.7 S cm-1 and a power factor of 35 µW m-1 K-2, among the highest values reported for n-doped molecular semiconductors.
