Thesis · Chapter 6 · Perspective

Technological assessment of organic thermoelectric generators

The concluding chapter of my PhD steps back from the bench and asks the uncomfortable question: are OTEGs a viable technology for waste heat recovery? I benchmark them against their real competitors — inorganic TEGs and the organic Rankine cycle — trace where device design, not chemistry, loses the plot, and use finite-element modelling to work out what a printed OTEG could actually become.

Manuscript under peer review — full text available on request

01 · The question

Materials are only half the answer

The field of organic thermoelectrics has seen substantial investment in the chemistry and physics of new materials, yet its technological maturity and future trajectory remain unclear. The academic literature largely assumes that organic semiconductors hold significant advantages over conventional thermoelectric materials, and that solving a few key challenges would unlock cheap and green energy.

This chapter tests that assumption. The method is deliberately unusual for a materials thesis: alongside the state of the art, I survey techno-economic analyses and life-cycle assessments — domains rarely examined in materials-focused research, but essential for evaluating practical potential.

02 · Application scope

Where TEGs make sense — and where they don't

Thermoelectric generators are silent, solid-state and scalable down — but their conversion efficiency is modest, so the application scope has to be chosen honestly. Body heat harvesting, the poster child of wearable thermoelectrics, is a poor fit: a human body dissipates roughly 6 mW cm-2 at a Carnot limit of 3.5%, and skin can only reject heat at ~10 W m-2 K-1. Once thermal transport is accounted for, the output drops well below 1 µW cm-2 — enough for the humblest sensor node, and not much else.

Industrial low-grade waste heat is the more realistic target: it is vast (over half of all waste heat is low-temperature), and the expansion of data centres is projected to add 945 TWh of extra-low-quality heat by 2030. But here TEGs face their real competitor, the organic Rankine cycle: a mature technology reaching 10–20% conversion efficiency at megawatt scale. Inorganic TEGs sit at 5–10% and only make sense where ORCs are impractical — small, distributed, low-power-density scenarios. OTEGs inherit that niche, with the added promise of printability.

03 · State of the field

Materials advanced. Devices didn't.

Recent years have seen remarkable progress in organic thermoelectric materials — power factors climbing steadily on both sides of the junction, up to a record room-temperature zT of 0.79. But when the lens moves to devices, the progress stalls. The normalized power densities of reported OTEGs span six orders of magnitude, and the best of them remain far from commercial inorganic modules.

The bottlenecks are engineering, not chemistry: suboptimal architectures, thermal management challenges, and the absence of standardized testing protocols. Consider one counterintuitive result: vertical structures should outperform planar ones — lower thermal resistance, higher fill fraction — yet in the literature the opposite happens.

04 · Case study

Anatomy of a printed OTEG

To make the architectural trade-offs concrete, we built an in silico replica of a scalable, fully printed — but not yet optimized — OTEG from the literature (Brunetti et al.): silver interconnects, screen-printed legs, a dielectric spacer, all on a thin polyimide substrate. Full 3D finite-element simulations, mesh-independent, with experimentally gathered material properties.

With a validated model in hand, the thickness of every component becomes a design dial — and each dial controls a different property.

05 · Optimization

What an optimized OTEG could be

Now the dials get turned in sequence. Swapping the silver ink for a scalable p-type polymer multiplies the normalized power density fivefold; a state-of-the-art material adds an order of magnitude; a thinner, higher-conductivity substrate does the same with no new chemistry at all; and photovoltaic-grade screen-printed interconnects — routine in silicon panel production — cut the internal resistance by nearly two orders of magnitude. Together, the NPD surpasses 5 µW K-2 cm-2, rivaling expensive high-performance inorganic commercial modules.

This is thermal impedance matching: a device must be designed for the heat exchanger it will live with, not for ideal infinite reservoirs. Thicker legs (100 µm to 10 mm) bring the requirements back to earth — at the cost of power output, and of screen-printing as the fabrication route. Extrusion, hot pressing and molding enter the picture. Every choice is a system-level negotiation between the active material, the inactive stack, and the world the device sits in.

06 · Economics

Lessons from organic photovoltaics

The closest analogue to OTEGs' journey is OPV: a flexible, printable organic technology that went from lab curiosity to pilot production by attacking cost and lifetime, not just efficiency. The economics hinge on synthetic complexity — material cost grows roughly linearly with each synthetic step, and high-performing OPV materials can need up to 20 of them. For context: of the two high-zT n-type polymers in this chapter, the 5-step PBDFO sells at ~205 €/g, while the 12-step PO-12 remains confined to lab-scale synthesis.

In OPV and OLED, standardized cost-to-performance metrics (i-FOM, cost per efficiency) let the community identify the best performance-per-euro trade-offs. The organic thermoelectrics community has yet to adopt them — nor the stability protocols that made OPV lifetimes comparable across labs. Until it does, it will keep optimizing materials whose synthesis can never scale.

07 · Conclusions

A field at its crossroads

OTEGs are not a failed technology — they are an unfinished engineering project. Material progress is real, but it has not translated into comparable device-level performance because too little attention has gone to the unglamorous parts: substrates, interconnects, heat exchangers, testing protocols, cost. The path forward is a shift from purely materials-driven research to a holistic approach — device engineering, economic feasibility, and real-world deployment strategies — so that OTEGs can find the niche applications where printed, flexible heat harvesters genuinely beat the alternatives.
