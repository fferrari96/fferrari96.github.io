Thesis · Chapter 6 · Perspective

Technological assessment of organic thermoelectric generators

The concluding chapter of my PhD steps back from the bench and asks the uncomfortable question: are OTEGs a viable technology for waste heat recovery? I benchmark them against their real competitors — inorganic TEGs and the organic Rankine cycle — trace where device design, not chemistry, loses the plot, and use finite-element modelling to work out what a printed OTEG could actually become.

01 · The question

Materials are only half the answer

The field of organic thermoelectrics has seen substantial investment in the chemistry and physics of new materials, yet its technological maturity and future trajectory remain unclear. The academic literature largely assumes that organic semiconductors hold significant advantages over conventional thermoelectric materials, and that solving a few key challenges would unlock cheap and green energy.

This chapter tests that assumption. The method is deliberately unusual for a materials thesis: alongside the state of the art, I survey techno-economic analyses and life-cycle assessments — domains rarely examined in materials-focused research, but essential for evaluating practical potential.

02 · Application scope

Where TEGs make sense — and where they don't

Thermoelectric generators are silent, solid-state and scalable down — but their conversion efficiency is modest, so the application scope has to be chosen honestly. Body heat harvesting, the poster child of wearable thermoelectrics, is a poor fit: a human body dissipates roughly 6 mW cm-2 at a Carnot limit of 3.5%, and skin can only reject heat at ~10 W m-2 K-1. Once thermal transport is accounted for, the output drops well below 1 µW cm-2 — enough for the humblest sensor node, and not much else.

Industrial low-grade waste heat is the more realistic target: it is vast (over half of all waste heat is low-temperature), and the expansion of data centres is projected to add 945 TWh of extra-low-quality heat by 2030. But here TEGs face their real competitor, the organic Rankine cycle: a mature technology reaching 10–20% conversion efficiency at megawatt scale. Inorganic TEGs sit at 5–10% and only make sense where ORCs are impractical — small, distributed, low-power-density scenarios. OTEGs inherit that niche, with the added promise of printability.

04 · Case study

A printed OTEG, taken apart

To see why devices lag behind their materials, it helps to take one apart. Chapter 5 of my thesis does exactly that: a fully screen-printed, flexible OTEG built to scale to square metres, paired with a finite-element digital twin. Two of its findings apply to the whole field. The 25 µm polyimide substrate takes most of the temperature difference before it ever reaches the legs. And the 3 µm silver interconnects both dominate the electrical resistance and thermally decouple the legs, which is why the textbook equivalent circuit predicts far less voltage than these devices actually deliver.

05 · Optimization

What an optimized OTEG could be

Starting from that printed device, the design choices get optimized one at a time. Swapping the silver ink for a scalable p-type polymer multiplies the normalized power density fivefold; a state-of-the-art material adds an order of magnitude; a thinner, higher-conductivity substrate does the same with no new chemistry at all; and photovoltaic-grade screen-printed interconnects — routine in silicon panel production — cut the internal resistance by nearly two orders of magnitude. Together, the NPD surpasses 5 µW K-2 cm-2, rivaling expensive high-performance inorganic commercial modules.

This is thermal impedance matching: a device must be designed for the heat exchanger it will live with, not for ideal infinite reservoirs. Thicker legs (100 µm to 10 mm) bring the requirements back to earth — at the cost of power output, and of screen-printing as the fabrication route. Extrusion, hot pressing and molding enter the picture. Every choice is a system-level negotiation between the active material, the inactive stack, and the world the device sits in.

06 · Cost and environmental impact

What it costs, in euros and in carbon

Efficiency alone does not decide whether a heat harvester gets built: cost per watt, environmental footprint and lifetime do. The chapter surveys the techno-economic analyses (TEAs) and life-cycle assessments (LCAs) of each competitor. None exist yet for OTEGs, so it borrows them from organic electronics that have already reached the market: displays, solar cells and transistors.

Inorganic TEGs against the Rankine cycle

The few techno-economic analyses of TEGs rarely agree. Commercial modules can cost up to 1000 €/W depending on the supplier, while cost models put them between 0.1 and 2 €/W. Several studies point at the same culprit: the heat exchanger, which made up ~60% of the cost of a screen-printed TEG integrated into a plate heat exchanger (4.7 €/W, or 0.04 €/kWh over 20 years at a 60 °C difference), and only ~13% in a survey of commercial systems. That survey also found costs falling below 1 €/W for larger systems. The useful pattern is how each technology scales down: the cost per watt of an ORC climbs steeply for small or low-quality heat sources, while that of a TEG changes gradually. Small and distributed is where TEGs can win.

The footprint of bismuth telluride

Life-cycle assessments break a TEG down into every step it takes to make one, and they keep finding the same thing: the impact lies in the processing, not the raw elements. Melting bismuth and tellurium into an alloy accounts for more than 88% of the global warming potential of a Bi2Te3 material; dicing the ingots and sintering the alumina plates are the most energy-intensive steps in commercial modules. Production scale changes the picture: in large runs the raw materials reach ~75% of a module's impact, in small runs the electric furnace dominates. Once the module is fitted to a car, the dominant material is not the thermoelectric but the steel of the heat exchanger. One study even rates Bi2Te3 processing as energy-efficient compared with other thermoelectrics, organic ones included. The lasting problem is supply: bismuth and antimony are on the European Commission's list of critical raw materials.

Organic devices: the molecules are the cheap part

For organics, the heat exchanger argument cuts both ways. Bolting cheap, low-performing organic legs onto a classical heat exchanger does not pay: the exchanger dominates the system cost, so the price of the active material barely matters and maximizing zT wins, even at zT 0.25. That weakens the case for OTEGs in large installations. But swapping exchangers and spreaders for cheap, flexible polymer substrates shifts the balance: at low heat flux, low-cost organic materials come out ahead.

Mature organic electronics show where an OTEG's costs would actually sit. In organic transistors, lithographic patterning with fluorinated chemicals carries the largest impact, followed by vacuum-deposited metal electrodes. In AMOLED displays, facility energy is the largest contributor (41.5% of the total energy), and ~80% of the energy for depositing the organic layers goes into the vacuum evaporation itself; the organic emitters are only 10–15% of a display's cost. In early organic solar cells, keeping an inert atmosphere was the biggest burden while the active semiconductors were under 1% of the energy demand, and printing in ambient air removed that penalty. The one LCA of a roll-to-roll printed OTEG agrees: the substrate takes most of the energy, then the screen printing of the active layer, and depositing the organic material costs over three times the energy embodied in it.

The price of a molecule

Once processing is under control, the active material sets the floor. The price of a conjugated semiconductor grows roughly linearly with each synthetic step, and high-performing OPV materials can need up to 20, which puts them at hundreds to thousands of euros per gram. Industrial scale-up pulls prices towards the materials-only cost, most of all for polymers with few steps. Of the two high-zT n-type polymers in this chapter, the 5-step PBDFO is sold at ~205 €/g, while the 12-step PO-12 is still made only at lab scale. OPV and OLED researchers rank materials by cost-to-performance metrics (the industrial figure of merit i-FOM, cost per efficiency); organic thermoelectrics has yet to adopt any.

Lifetime and end of life

A cheap device still has to last. OTEG stability is rarely studied and never with a shared method: no report gives a standard metric such as t80, and nothing like the ISOS protocols that made OPV lifetimes comparable across labs exists yet. The data so far is encouraging, with devices stable for up to 200 days in ambient air. OPV again shows the payoff: energy and carbon payback times less than half those of multicrystalline silicon, and at end of life the panels can be safely incinerated with energy recovery instead of becoming toxic electronic waste. Unlike OPV, OTEGs do not face a giant incumbent: inorganic TEGs are themselves a niche. The task is to find the applications where a light, flexible, printed harvester does what rigid modules cannot.

07 · Conclusions

A field at its crossroads

OTEGs are not a failed technology — they are an unfinished engineering project. Material progress is real, but it has not translated into comparable device-level performance because too little attention has gone to the unglamorous parts: substrates, interconnects, heat exchangers, testing protocols, cost. The path forward is a shift from purely materials-driven research to a holistic approach — device engineering, economic feasibility, and real-world deployment strategies — so that OTEGs can find the niche applications where printed, flexible heat harvesters genuinely beat the alternatives.
