Side project · in progress

A test bench for photodiodes

An automated bench that measures up to eight commercial photodiodes, one at a time, under an LED and, once the heater is in, at temperatures up to 85 °C. I am designing and building all of it myself: the electronics, the firmware, the Python that runs the measurements and, next, the 3D-printed mechanics.

Why

A sketch from a previous job, built for real

At a previous job I sketched a bench for ageing photodiodes under light and heat: a holder for the diodes with a heater under it. That was the whole idea, and I never got to build it. When I picked it up again on my own, there was no lab instrument to read the diodes with, so the readout had to be built too. That part, the analog electronics, has taken most of the time so far, and it is the one I learn the most from: it is my first real design with operational amplifiers.

The goal is narrow for now: measure commercial photodiodes in open circuit, short circuit and reverse bias, at different light levels and temperatures, automatically, with as little hand work per diode as possible, on a bench compact enough to move in and out of a glove box easily, so that it can later measure air-sensitive devices without taking them out of the inert atmosphere. Current–voltage sweeps and measuring LEDs as well come later, and the design leaves room for both.

It is also my way to learn three things properly: analog electronics, from breadboard to PCB, assemblies in FreeCAD, and the software that ties an instrument to its data. As with the conductivity rig from my PhD, the point is to stop measuring one device at a time: here, eight photodiodes share one holder and one readout.

How it works

One readout, eight diodes, three modes

A laptop runs each measurement as a Python script and talks to an Arduino Nano over USB, one short text command per line. The Nano switches relays and reads converters; it holds no measurement logic of its own. An 8-channel relay board connects one photodiode at a time to the readout and keeps the others in short circuit. The readout puts that diode in one of three modes and turns its current into a voltage for a 16-bit ADC.

Mode 1

Open circuit, Voc

The diode's voltage with no current drawn. A follower reads the cathode without loading it. It has to be a CMOS op amp: an LM358 draws about 45 nA into its input, enough to pull Voc down in dim light.

Mode 2

Short circuit, Isc

Both ends of the diode sit at a 2 V reference, so it sees no voltage, and a transimpedance amplifier turns the photocurrent into a voltage: Vout = Vref − I·Rf. The closest to how a photodiode is used.

Mode 3

Reverse bias, −3 V

A relay moves the cathode to the 5 V rail through 1 kΩ, about 3 V above the reference. Same amplifier, same reading; this is the mode where dark current and leakage show up.

Design choices

The reasons behind each choice

The firmware only follows orders

One general firmware on the Nano answers one-line commands (set a pin, read a pin, switch the ADC reference), and every measurement is a Python script on the laptop, so changing a measurement never means reflashing. A command and its reply take about 1.8 ms; anything that needs timing tighter than about 10 ms belongs in the firmware, because Windows adds jitter.

The amplifier sits on the low side

The LM358 I started with cannot drive its output above about 3.5 V on a 5 V supply. With the transimpedance amplifier on the cathode, reverse bias had only about 0.3 V of room. On the anode, referenced at 2 V, the output swings down instead, with about 2 V of room in both current modes.

Gain by relay, never an open loop

1 MΩ stays in the feedback at all times; two small signal relays add 100 kΩ or 10 kΩ in parallel, for ranges of about 2, 20 and 200 µA. Fixed 1 % resistors rather than a potentiometer, so the gain is known and a script can autorange, and the loop is never open while a relay moves.

Light from a current, not a duty cycle

The LED driver is a constant-current sink set by a DAC: steady DC, not PWM, so the photodiode sees constant light, and a known current, so the same bench can later characterize LEDs as well as photodiodes. The current does not depend on the LED's forward voltage, so LEDs of different colours swap without changing parts, and two sense resistors (47 Ω and 2.2 kΩ) cover about 20 mA down to a few µA.

Relay coils on their own supply

Switching one relay moved the 2 V reference by 18 mV: the coil current was returning through the ground the analog parts share. With the coils on a second USB supply and their return kept apart, the reference now holds within 3 mV in every relay state.

Seven stages, each checked before the next

The main board went together on two breadboards in seven stages, each with its own test, and each checked against the schematic's netlist before the first wire went in. On the way it caught a reversed supply, two swapped gate wires and a batch of op amps that turned out to be faulty.

Where it stands

Six stages working, one to go

  1. Supply and Nano Done
  2. I²C converters Done

    An ADS1115 ADC and two MCP4725 DACs; each DAC stepped from zero to full scale and read back on both ADCs.

  3. 2 V reference Done

    A buffered divider: 2.02 V, steady while the DACs step.

  4. LED driver On / off only

    The current loop oscillated. For now the two range resistors set the LED at 0.9 mA or 10 mA, and the regulation comes back once the rest works.

  5. Coil supply and mode relays Done

    Two relays select Voc, Isc or −3 V; coils on their own supply.

  6. Amplifier and gain relays Works, with LM358

    Output within 1 mV of the reference at all three gains. The MCP6002 op amps I bought online were faulty or not genuine; real CMOS parts are next on the list.

  7. 8-channel relay board Next

After the breadboard

  • A 100 × 100 mm two-layer PCB for the main board: the schematic is drawn in KiCad, the board waits until the breadboard is complete.
  • The mechanics, designed in FreeCAD and 3D printed: a holder that presses eight diodes onto spring-loaded pogo pins under a latched lid, an LED stand with a rack and pinion to set the height and a diffuser for even light, a base plate with magnets, and a case for the electronics.
  • A heater and a temperature sensor under the holder, up to 85 °C for accelerated ageing.
  • Then the first real run: Voc and Isc of eight diodes against light level and temperature.
  • Later: full current–voltage sweeps instead of three fixed points, and LEDs measured against their drive current.
